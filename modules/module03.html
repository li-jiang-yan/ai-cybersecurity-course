THE LECTURE · 35 MIN

Think in systems, not just models

A capable tool can still be the wrong tool

A model may produce fluent, plausible answers without knowing whether they are true. A task that needs exact arithmetic, current facts, consistent policy, or accountability may require ordinary software, verified sources, or a qualified person. Good product design starts with the user’s problem, not with the availability of a model.

Automation bias and the confidence trap

People tend to accept an automated suggestion, especially when they are busy or the output sounds authoritative. This can turn a small model error into a real decision. A reviewer who merely clicks “approve” is not meaningful oversight. Reviewers need context, time, authority to disagree, and a way to verify the answer.

A simple suitability check

Ask what happens when the model is wrong, who can notice, whether the outcome can be reversed, and how often a person can realistically review it. The more severe and irreversible the harm, the stronger the verification and human authority must be. Sometimes the right decision is not to automate.

CASE NOTE · A RÉSUMÉ-RANKING ASSISTANT

A company uses a language model to rank applicants and assumes the top-ranked résumé is the best candidate. The model may favor familiar wording, miss unusual career paths, or reflect patterns in its examples. If a recruiter sees only a score, the system can quietly narrow who gets considered. A safer design treats the output as a documented, contestable signal and checks outcomes across groups.

YOUR WORKSHEET

Run an AI suitability review

Write in the space below or use paper. Nothing is sent or saved.

15 MIN
  1. Choose one task: drafting a meeting summary, screening job applicants, triaging medical symptoms, or approving expense reimbursements.
  2. Score each dimension from 1 (low) to 3 (high): consequence of error, difficulty of verification, irreversibility, and pressure to act quickly.
  3. Write what evidence a human reviewer would need to verify the output. If the answer is “the model’s confidence score,” add independent evidence.
  4. Choose one role: automate, recommend for review, assist with drafting only, or do not use AI. Explain the choice.
  5. Name a fallback when the model is unavailable, uncertain, or challenged by an affected person.

Stuck? Open the worked solution
  • For résumé ranking, the consequence and verification difficulty can be high, and the decision may affect a person’s opportunity.
  • Use the system only to assist with organizing applications against explicit job criteria; do not auto-reject based on a generated score.
  • Give reviewers the original résumé and the specific evidence behind any flag. Audit false positives and outcomes across relevant groups.
  • Let applicants correct information or request human review. If the tool is unavailable, return to the established human process.
  • For a low-impact, reversible task such as a meeting-summary draft, AI can have a larger role if participants can correct errors and sensitive content is handled appropriately.

END OF MODULE 03

Keep these ideas

  • Start with the task and failure cost, then decide whether AI belongs.
  • Fluent wording and confidence scores do not prove correctness.
  • Human oversight needs time, evidence, authority, and an appeal path.
  • Provide a useful manual fallback and make uncertainty visible.
