THE LECTURE · 35 MIN

A field that needs careful curiosity

The system keeps changing

Models are becoming multimodal, more capable at coding, and more connected to tools and other agents. This can improve assistance and speed, while making behavior harder to predict and increasing the consequences of excessive permissions. Security work must keep pace with capabilities, deployment patterns, and new ways people rely on systems.

Research questions worth asking

Can we reliably tell whether a model is following a trusted instruction or untrusted content? What evidence can prove a model was trained or served as claimed? How can we measure robustness across realistic changes in language, images, and context? How do many agents fail when they share tools or memory? How can users contest and correct decisions made with AI? These questions combine technical research with governance and human factors.

A durable skill set

Strong AI security work combines foundations in software security, machine learning, threat modeling, privacy, and communication. You do not need to master everything first. Start by reading system diagrams carefully, stating assumptions, reproducing a small safe experiment, and explaining the limit of each result. Curiosity and disciplined skepticism are useful tools.

CASE NOTE · FROM BROAD CONCERN TO A RESEARCHABLE QUESTION

“Make AI secure” is too large to study. “For a fixed RAG prototype with a public document set, how does a permission-aware retrieval filter affect unauthorized-document disclosure under a defined set of benign and adversarial queries?” is more testable. A good question names a system, threat model, measure, and boundary. It also avoids claiming that a benchmark proves real-world security.

YOUR WORKSHEET

Draft a mini research proposal

Write in the space below or use paper. Nothing is sent or saved.

20 MIN
  1. Choose a topic: RAG prompt injection, model extraction, data poisoning, AI code review, or multi-agent permissions.
  2. Write one research question that names the system and attacker capability.
  3. Choose a safe test environment and data source. Use a toy model, synthetic data, or a local sandbox; never target a service without permission.
  4. Define one primary metric and one side effect to monitor, such as answer utility, false refusal, latency, or privacy leakage.
  5. Write the strongest limitation that could make your result misleading, and one follow-up experiment.

Stuck? Open the worked solution
  • Example question: “On a toy RAG application with synthetic policy documents, how much does permission-aware retrieval reduce cross-user disclosure under a fixed set of direct and indirect prompt-injection tests?”
  • Attacker capability: a user can submit questions and control one document in the test corpus, but cannot access the application host.
  • Environment: local, isolated demo with synthetic user accounts and fake records.
  • Metrics: unauthorized disclosure rate, correct-answer rate on benign requests, and false refusals.
  • Limitation: synthetic documents and a small model may not represent production data or larger systems.
  • Follow-up: vary the retrieval policy and test whether results persist across a second model and paraphrased attacks.

END OF MODULE 07

Keep these ideas

  • More capable and connected systems create new risks and new research opportunities.
  • Strong research states assumptions, measures outcomes, and names its limits.
  • Safe experiments use synthetic data and authorized sandboxes.
  • Core skills include threat modeling, software security, ML literacy, careful measurement, and clear communication.
