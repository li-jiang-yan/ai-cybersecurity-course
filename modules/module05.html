THE LECTURE · 40 MIN

Think in systems, not just models

Incidents are systems stories

A breach report often begins with a visible event: data appeared in an answer, an employee followed an impersonation, or an agent used a tool unexpectedly. Investigation asks what enabled it: exposed permissions, weak identity checks, missing logs, excessive access, or an unsafe process. Avoid blaming “the AI” when several controls failed together.

Model leaks and extraction

A model may expose memorized training details or confidential context accidentally included in a prompt. Repeated probing may reveal behavior or approximate a service. A leak may come from the model, but it may also be a direct database or logging failure. Keep secrets out of prompts, isolate tenants, minimize retention, and monitor unusual query patterns.

Deepfakes and agents

Synthetic voice or video can make an urgent request appear to come from a trusted colleague. The robust response is process-based: verify through a second known channel, use approval thresholds for high-value actions, and never make identity depend on appearance alone. Agents add another link: they can turn persuasive text into actions through legitimate tools, so permission boundaries and confirmation steps matter.

CASE NOTE · A VIDEO CALL AND AN URGENT TRANSFER

In 2024, Hong Kong police described a case in which an employee at engineering firm Arup was deceived during a video call featuring deepfake versions of colleagues; roughly US$25 million was transferred. The lesson is not that every call is fake. It is that video is not strong proof of identity for high-impact requests. Independent verification and payment controls can interrupt the chain.

YOUR WORKSHEET

Build an incident timeline and controls

Write in the space below or use paper. Nothing is sent or saved.

20 MIN
  1. Use the scenario: a finance employee joins a video call; a familiar voice requests an urgent confidential transfer; the employee approves it.
  2. Write the sequence as four stages: preparation, contact, decision, impact. Mark which details are known from the scenario and which are assumptions.
  3. At each stage, add one control that would prevent, detect, or limit harm.
  4. Choose a detection signal and name who receives the alert.
  5. Write a short post-incident question that would reveal whether the control actually worked.

Stuck? Open the worked solution
  • Preparation: attacker gathers public voice/video samples (scenario assumption). Reduce exposed executive media where feasible and educate staff about impersonation risks.
  • Contact: urgent video-call request. Verification control: call the requester back using a trusted directory number, not a number supplied in the request.
  • Decision: transfer approval. Require dual authorization and a documented out-of-band confirmation for high-value or unusual transfers.
  • Impact: money leaves the account. Set transaction thresholds, delays, and a rapid recall/escalation path.
  • Detection: alert finance security when a new payee and urgent exception coincide; assign an on-call owner.
  • Test: run a tabletop with a simulated urgent request and see if the employee uses the independent channel and approval process.

END OF MODULE 05

Keep these ideas

  • Incidents usually combine technical, procedural, and human factors.
  • A model leak may originate in retrieval, logging, permissions, or memorization.
  • Deepfakes weaken familiar identity cues; verify high-impact requests independently.
  • Agents can translate malicious content into action through their valid permissions.
